My research sits at the intersection of corporate strategy, innovation, and corporate governance. I study how different types of owners — typically private equity, corporate acquirers, and activist investors — reshape how firms create value and innovate. Drafts are available upon request.

Under Review

Beyond Capital: How Firms Create Value from Shareholders
with Mark DesJardine (equal contribution) · Under review at Strategic Management Journal
Abstract

Research on ownership largely treats shareholders as exogenous claimants on firm value, emphasizing how firms create value for shareholders rather than through them. Yet, in practice, managers influence who owns their firms and how much value they can realize from their shareholders. We develop a theory that reconceptualizes shareholders as sources of competitive advantage. Central to our theory is owner selection capability, which enables firms to identify shareholders whose competencies complement organizational needs. Exercising this capability allows firms to generate three advantages from owners: resource advantages (enhancing internal capabilities), brokerage advantages (bridging industry and geographic boundaries), and stakeholder advantages (influencing non-market actors). We aggregate these advantages to quantify the incremental value shareholders can contribute beyond capital depending on their configuration.

Working Papers

Preserving Commercialization vs. Integrating Technology: An Empirical Investigation of Technology Acquisitions by Private Equity and Corporate Acquirers
Preparing for submission to Strategic Management Journal
Abstract

This study examines how acquirer type shapes innovation at acquired technology firms, comparing private equity (PE) and corporate acquirers within a common framework. Because PE acquirers are financial owners that lack the complementary assets that corporate acquirers use to integrate a target, I argue that the two should select and reshape targets in different ways. Studying 375 PE and 2,803 corporate acquisitions of U.S. technology firms from 1990 to 2022, I find that PE acquirers select older, more commercially established targets while corporate acquirers select younger, more technically accomplished ones. After the deal, corporate-acquired targets decline across all innovation dimensions, whereas PE-acquired targets preserve their commercial output and reduce technical output far less. The dimensions on which each acquirer selects are the dimensions it reshapes. Acquirer type is thus a first-order determinant of target innovation, and selection and post-acquisition outcomes are two views of a single strategic logic.

Capital Efficiency and Innovation Capability: Private Equity’s Dual Imperatives in Technology Acquisitions
Preparing for submission to Organization Science
Abstract

Private equity has become an increasingly active player in technology acquisitions in recent years, yet most prior scholarship has focused on the effects of corporate acquirer ownership on performance and innovation outcomes. As a result, research provides little guidance on how technology firms should choose between the two acquirer types. To remedy this gap, I construct a panel data set of acquisitions in the chemical, biopharmaceutical, and medical device industries between 1990 and 2019, tracked yearly through 2022. I argue that, unlike corporate acquirers, private equity owners must navigate a tension between deploying capital efficiently and maintaining the target’s capacity to innovate, and that they resolve this tension differently from deal to deal. Measuring new product introductions from USPTO trademark filings, I find that both private equity and corporate acquirers reduce product innovation relative to matched non-acquired controls, but that the decline under private equity is substantially and significantly smaller than under corporate acquirers. Private equity’s effect, moreover, depends on the strategy an owner pursues: capital-efficiency strategies such as buy-and-build acquisitions deepen the post-acquisition decline, whereas capability-oriented strategies – secondary buyouts, the hiring of commercial-launch leadership, and the retention of the target’s inventors – soften it. These findings revise the prevailing view that private equity is uniformly detrimental to innovation, showing instead that ownership form, and the strategy an owner brings to a target, shapes the innovative trajectory of acquired technology firms.

Team Capital and Inventor Productivity After Private Equity and Corporate Acquisitions
Preparing for submission to Management Science
Abstract

Inventors carry productive capital not in themselves alone but in the teams within which they have learned to work. I ask what happens to this team capital when a technology firm is acquired, and whether the answer depends on the buyer. Corporate acquisitions have been studied at the inventor level and private equity (PE) at the firm level, but the two have never been compared head-to-head in a single inventor-level design. I assemble an inventor-year panel of U.S. acquisitions in chemicals, biopharmaceuticals, and medical devices from 1990 to 2022, match acquired inventors to similar non-acquired inventors, and split each inventor’s post-deal output into work done at the acquired firm and work done elsewhere. The two buyers move inventor output in opposite directions. Under PE, output at the acquired firm holds steady while the most capable inventors produce more elsewhere; under corporate ownership, output outside the firm is unchanged while quality falls among the inventors who stay. Team capital is the channel, and its two dimensions behave differently. The breadth of an inventor’s prior collaboration is portable and sharpens each pattern, whereas the depth of repeated collaboration with the same colleagues is not, and an acquisition erodes it under either owner. Team capital is therefore governance-sensitive, and what survives a change in ownership is the portable part of an inventor’s collaborative capital rather than the relationship-specific part that made a particular team productive in the first place.

Corporate Power, Social Movements, and Shareholder Democracy
with Mary-Hunter McDonnell · Preparing for submission to Strategic Management Journal
Abstract

Drawing on theories of power, embeddedness, and opportunity structures, we argue that corporate power shapes who wins contested fights between activists and companies over the legitimacy of firm actions. We present 2 theoretical extensions to the literature: (1) We conceptualize corporate opportunities as embedded within political opportunity structures (2) We introduce a new theoretical edifice of corporate power consisting of Transposing, Contextual, and Conflicting Power. We test our theory within the context of shareholder voting and find broad support for our framework. In particular, we find that a 1 unit increase in a corporation’s Tobin’s Q (economic power) is associated with both 21% higher odds of activists winning challenges at the SEC and 2.1% less vote share for activist resolutions at annual meetings.
